AGR2026: Predicting Crop Yield Across Uganda's Subregions

A structural analysis of soil, climate, geography, infrastructure, and population data across Uganda's 14 UBOS subregions.

Why This Project

Uganda's northeastern region, Karamoja, is living through a severe hunger crisis as of 2026 -- the government has launched emergency relief food for 313,987 households across the region's nine districts[1], and a Food Rights Alliance official told Al Jazeera that crop losses now range from at least 50 to 70 percent in most areas[2]. This isn't the first time: the Uganda Human Rights Commission's 2022 report recorded at least 2,207 hunger-related deaths in Karamoja that year[3]. Researchers expect more of the same -- as Kristof Titeca of the University of Antwerp put it, "it will unfortunately not be the last"[2].

This project started from a simple question: can publicly available data about soil, climate, and local conditions help explain why some regions consistently struggle to grow enough food, while others don't? The map further down this page, showing water deficit by region, lines up with where this crisis is hitting hardest -- Karamoja, the region in the news, is among the driest subregions on the map, alongside West Nile, Acholi and Teso.

To be upfront about what this is and isn't: this is early-stage research covering 16 crops (maize, beans, cassava, groundnuts, two coffee types, millet, sorghum, rice, sweet and Irish potatoes, simsim, three banana types and soya beans), and the models here are not accurate enough to act on directly. Only a few crops show a signal that more than one independent model agrees on, and even there the drivers are often unclear. What this project can do, honestly, is show that the pattern is real and explainable with data -- and lay groundwork that, expanded with more years of data, could eventually support real decisions. That expansion is the plan for where this project goes next.

Feature Glossary

Plain-language definitions of the key variables behind this project's models:

  • def (water deficit): how much drier an area is than its plants need -- the gap between the moisture crops could use and what the soil and rain actually provide. The single strongest predictor found in this project.
  • ppt: rainfall (precipitation).
  • tmin / tmax: average minimum and maximum temperature.
  • vap / vpd: measures of air moisture (humidity-related).
  • soc (soil organic carbon): a measure of soil fertility and humus content.
  • clay / cec / phh2o / cfvo: soil texture, nutrient-holding capacity, acidity, and stone content -- all affecting how well soil supports plant growth.
  • slope / aspect: steepness and compass-direction of the land, affecting drainage, erosion, and sunlight exposure.
  • pct_any_electricity / pct_radio / pct_improved_water: the share of households in a subregion with access to electricity, radio, and safe drinking water -- used as proxies for general infrastructure and farmer access to information and resources.
  • drivable_density_km_per_km2: density of drivable roads, a measure of market access.
  • households / avg_hh_size: population and household size context for each subregion.

Methodology

We looked at 16 crops -- maize, beans, cassava, groundnuts, two coffee types, millet, sorghum, rice, sweet and Irish potatoes, simsim, three banana types and soya beans -- grown across Uganda's 14 regions over several survey years, and tried to understand how much of the difference in how well each crop grows from one region to another can be explained by things like rainfall, soil quality, temperature, how hilly the land is, and how connected a region is (roads, electricity, radio access).

To make sure we weren't fooling ourselves, We tested each region's prediction using only information learned from the other 13 regions -- the model was never allowed to "peek" at the answer for the region being tested. We also cross-checked every result with four different analysis methods (Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, a simple decision tree and Ridge regression), a bit like confirming a story with several independent sources: a finding only counts as trustworthy if more than one of them agrees on it. When we later tuned each method's settings, we did it inside the same test -- choosing settings using only the training regions, never the region being tested -- so tuning could not quietly flatter the scores.

One caution applies throughout: variables like access to improved water, electricity or radio often rank as the strongest predictors, but they most likely stand in for things we cannot measure, such as farmer wealth, rather than acting on yield directly. A model that predicts well is not the same as a model that has found the cause.

Some results came out shaky, unclear, or simply didn't hold up -- and rather than hide that, we're reporting it honestly. A result that doesn't work is still useful information.

Coffee: Robusta & Arabica (Added in Phase 2)

Coffee is Uganda's largest export crop, and was added to this project after our initial four staples (maize, beans, cassava, groundnuts) -- both Robusta and Arabica, Uganda's two commercially grown types.

Building this dataset surfaced real problems worth being upfront about: the original government source data for coffee had a column-alignment error that silently produced wrong production figures, and one official report table for Arabica in 2021/22 turned out to be an exact duplicate of the Robusta table for the same year -- a genuine error in the source document. Both were caught and corrected by rebuilding the affected tables directly from the original published reports, cross-checked against the report's own reported yield figures row by row.

See the "All Crops at a Glance" section above for yield, production, and trend charts covering this crop alongside all others.

On the modeling attempt: unlike the four original crops, we were not able to build a reliable predictive model for coffee yield. Random Forest and Ridge regression results disagreed with each other and, in several tests, performed worse than simply guessing the average yield. The most likely reason is sample size: with only 26-41 subregion-year data points spread across 14 subregions (compared to 42-56 for the original crops), there usually isn't enough data per subregion for a reliable model, especially for Arabica. Rather than present an unreliable model as if it were trustworthy, we are showing the underlying data honestly instead.

See the "All Crops at a Glance" section above for yield, production, and trend charts covering this crop alongside all others.

Cereals: Millet, Sorghum & Rice (Phase 2, continued)

These three grain staples were added together as a batch, following coffee. As with coffee, building this dataset surfaced real source-data problems: the 2020 government report used a more complex table layout for several crops, and this caused the original parsing to silently fail or misalign data for multiple crops in that year -- not just one. Sorghum's entire 2020 table was missing outright; millet and rice each had partial 2020 data that, on closer inspection, had rows swapped between subregions (one subregion's real numbers were filed under a different subregion's name). All three were corrected by re-transcribing the 2020 tables directly from the original report, row by row, cross-checked against the report's own computed yield figures.

A note on reading the charts above: "yield" (measured in mt/ha) and "production" (measured in total MT) are different things. Yield measures how productive each hectare of land is; production measures the total harvest. A subregion can have high yield (very efficient land use) while a *different* subregion produces far more total grain simply because it plants a much larger area, even at lower efficiency per hectare. Karamoja is a clear example for sorghum: it plants a much larger total area than smaller subregions, so despite having a lower yield per hectare, it is likely a far larger overall producer. Both charts are shown to avoid misleading conclusions from either one alone.

See the "All Crops at a Glance" section above for yield, production, and trend charts covering this crop alongside all others.

On the modeling results: of the three cereals, only rice produced a reliable finding: three independently-built models (Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, and a simple decision tree) agreed that rainfall and climatic water deficit are real drivers of rice yield -- drier conditions mean lower yield. For sorghum, three of the four models beat the average (Random Forest, Ridge and, after tuning, Gradient Boosting), and Random Forest and Ridge stay positive when any single subregion is left out -- so sorghum is predictable, but we are not presenting a driver: the tree models' top feature is access to improved water, probably a stand-in for farmer wealth, and only rainfall was named by all four models. For millet, only Ridge clearly beat the average (Random Forest only marginally, the other two did worse than guessing), so we treat it as unconfirmed. The data is shown above regardless, because raw data is useful on its own even without a reliable model behind it.

Irish Potatoes, Sweet Potatoes & Simsim (Phase 2, continued)

These three crops were added as a batch, continuing the same systematic check we now run on every new crop: we found the 2020 government report's tables for all three were missing from our earlier parsed data, caused by the same complex 2020 table layout that disrupted coffee, sorghum, millet, and rice parsing. This time, rather than re-transcribing from screenshots table by table, we worked directly from an extracted text version of the full report, locating and cross-checking each table against the report's own computed yield figures — the same validation standard as before, just a faster path to it.

We also tested adding farming-practice data (irrigation use, pesticide use, fertilizer use, improved seed use) to see if it explained more of the yield pattern for these crops. It didn't: merging this data caused roughly half of our usable records to drop out (due to missing survey responses), and results got worse or unstable, not better. We reverted to the original feature set rather than keep a model built on a shrunken, less representative sample.

On the modeling results: Irish potatoes is this project's cleanest null result yet — all four models tested (Random Forest, Ridge, Gradient Boosting, and a simple decision tree) agreed there is no reliable yield driver in this feature set. For sweet potatoes and simsim, results were weak and inconsistent across models, so, consistent with how every inconclusive result in this project is treated, we are not presenting a driver for either. As with coffee and the cereals, the underlying yield, production, and trend data for all three crops is still shown honestly in the unified charts above — a visual pattern can be informative even without a model behind it.

Banana (Food, Beer & Sweet) and Soya Beans (Phase 2, continued)

These four crops rounded out this phase of the project. As with earlier batches, the same 2020 government report table-layout issue surfaced again — this time as two different problems. Soya beans and banana-food had their 2020 data present but internally scrambled: production and area figures were shifted into the wrong columns and, for banana-food, subregion labels were cyclically mismatched with their real values. Banana-sweet and banana-beer were missing 2020 entirely from the original parsed data. All four were corrected by re-extracting directly from the source report and cross-validating against its own reported formulas, the same standard applied to every crop in this project. Banana-sweet and banana-beer also turned out to only have reliable data for two survey years (2019 and 2020) rather than four — not a parsing failure, just thinner source coverage for these crops specifically, so results for these two should be read with that smaller sample in mind.

On the modeling results: banana-food is this phase's most interesting finding — all four models (Random Forest, Ridge, Gradient Boosting, and a decision tree) agree its yield is predictable from the feature set. But what drives it is genuinely unclear: Random Forest points to water access and soil carbon, Ridge points to soil texture and slope, with little overlap between them. That's the same confounding pattern seen earlier with cassava and coffee robusta — infrastructure variables often proxy for farmer wealth rather than directly causing yield — so we're reporting banana-food as predictable, not as having an identified cause. Soya beans and banana-sweet are confirmed null results: all four models agree there's no reliable signal in this feature set. Banana-beer is unconfirmed: two of four models found a positive signal, two didn't, and its sample size (19 rows) is the smallest in the project.

One data quality note from this batch: a 2020 soya beans reading for Ankole subregion (376 MT from just 30 hectares harvested, a 12.4 mt/ha yield) is far outside every other subregion's 0.3-1.3 mt/ha range. We've kept it in the dataset rather than silently removing it, but flag it here as a likely data or survey error worth independent investigation rather than a genuine finding.

Model Performance by Crop

How to read the chart above: each bar shows how well one model family predicted yield for subregions it had never seen (leave-one-subregion-out testing). Zero means no better than guessing the average; a bar below zero means the model did worse than guessing the average; higher (up to 1) means it explained more of the difference between regions. All four model families are shown for all 16 crops. The first dropdown switches between default settings (what we originally reported) and settings tuned inside the cross-validation; the second zooms in, because a few bars (coffee, sweet potatoes, Irish potatoes) fall far below -1 and would otherwise flatten everything else. Hover over any bar for its exact value.

What the chart shows:
  • Maize, beans and rice -- more than one independently built model beats the average (beans: all four under default settings; rice: all four once tuned; maize: Random Forest and Ridge, plus Decision Tree after tuning). These are the crops where we report a driver.
  • Predictable, but the driver is unconfirmed: cassava, banana (food) and sorghum -- most models beat the average (cassava: three of four by default, four after tuning; banana food: all four; sorghum: three of four), but the models do not tell a consistent story about why. Infrastructure variables such as access to improved water often rank near the top, probably standing in for farmer wealth rather than acting on yield directly.
  • Weak or unconfirmed: millet, simsim, banana (beer) -- for millet only Ridge clearly beats the average; for simsim two of four models do. Banana beer is positive for all four models after tuning, but it has just 19 data points and one model fell from about 0.50 to 0.05 when tuned, which is too unstable to trust.
  • No reliable signal: groundnuts, both coffees, soya beans, sweet potatoes, banana (sweet) and Irish potatoes -- every model scores below zero, or (Irish potatoes) at best about 0.1.
On tuning: it did not create signal -- every crop where all four models scored below zero under default settings is still below zero after tuning. It can also hurt when the sample is tiny (banana beer, and Ridge on banana food, scored lower tuned than default), which is why both views are shown rather than only the more flattering one. Even the best scores here are moderate and rest on 19 to 56 data points per crop, so none of these models is accurate enough to act on directly.

Appendix: Data Sources

  • Crop yield data: Uganda Bureau of Statistics (UBOS), Annual Agricultural Survey (AAS) reports, 2018-2020, and the Uganda National Household Integrated Survey (UHIS) 2021/22 report, along with UBOS population and infrastructure data — ubos.org/explore-statistics.
  • Climate data: TerraClimate (University of Idaho / Climatology Lab), gridded monthly climate and water-balance data — climate.northwestknowledge.net.

  • Geography/terrain data: Copernicus Digital Elevation Model, via AWS Open Data — registry.opendata.aws/copernicus-dem.
  • Subregion/district boundaries: UNOSAT administrative boundary data for Uganda — unosat-geodrr.cern.ch.
